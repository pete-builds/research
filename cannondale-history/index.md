## TL;DR

Cannondale began in Wilton, Connecticut in 1971 making a bicycle trailer and bags. In 1983 it bet on oversized aluminum frames and, through the 1990s, became one of the most inventive and successful bike makers in the world. A late-1990s gamble on motocross bikes and ATVs lost tens of millions and pushed it into bankruptcy in January 2003. Since then it has had three owners: Pegasus Capital, Dorel Industries and, from 2022, the Dutch group Pon. Today, 21 October 2026, Cannondale announced it will close its Wilton headquarters by mid-2027 and move its global HQ to the Netherlands. Founder Joe Montgomery died in January 2026, so the brand leaves its hometown in the same year it lost the man who started it.

## Current Status

- **Today (21 Oct 2026): Wilton HQ is closing.** The global HQ moves to Woudenberg, Netherlands, and R&D stays in Freiburg, Germany. A Connecticut WARN notice lists 143 workers, with separations from 21 Dec 2026 through 31 Dec 2027 ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2026/10/21/cannondale-closing-connecticut-headquarters-consolidating-other-pon-brands)).
- **The new North American office is not settled.** Bicycle Retailer names Santa Cruz, California. Cannondale told Escape Collective the West Coast location "has not been decided" ([source](https://escapecollective.com/cannondale-will-close-its-us-headquarters/)).
- **2026 cut back manufacturing and racing.** Pon is closing the Cannondale factory in Almelo, Netherlands, with about 120 jobs lost ([source](https://road.cc/news/120-employees-laid-off-as-cannondale-production-facility-to-close)). Cannondale Factory Racing, its mountain bike team, ends after the 2026 season ([source](https://www.endurancesportswire.com/cannondale-to-shut-down-factory-race-program/)).
- **Product is still moving.** A new SuperSix EVO launched on 17 Feb 2026 ([source](https://www.bikeradar.com/news/2026-cannondale-supersix-evo)) and the CAAD14 aluminum race bike in March 2026 ([source](https://bicyclingaustralia.com.au/bikes/cannondales-caad14-returns-to-its-racing-roots/)).
- **The road sponsorship continues.** EF Education-EasyPost rides Cannondale under a 2023 deal "without an end date" ([source](https://www.efprocycling.com/culture/cannondale-and-ef-pro-cycling-extend-partnership/)). No 2027 bike switch has been reported.
- **Founder Joe Montgomery died on 2 Jan 2026,** aged 86 ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

## Findings

### Part 1: A trailer, a train station and a pay phone (1971 to 1982)

Joseph Stephen Montgomery was born on 11 December 1939 in Coshocton, Ohio. His father developed a process for coating cotton gloves with rubber, and his mother ran the family fruit farm ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). He tinkered from boyhood: at 12 he built a small jeep using a junkyard transmission, and his daughter later suggested a crash into a church's front doors "is how he ended up in boarding school" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

He never finished college. In his twenties he crewed racing sailboats in the Caribbean. His daughter says the masts gave him "an appreciation for the strength-to-weight aspect of aluminum", and so did an accident that left him and four others clinging to an overturned aluminum hull ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). After "stints in finance and running a bar/restaurant in New York City," he founded Cannondale ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). "I don't have a college degree, but I've always been a seat-of-the-pants engineer type," he told The New York Times in 1993 ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

**The founding.** Cannondale was incorporated in Delaware in 1971, and Montgomery was its chairman, president and CEO from the start ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)). The NYT obituary says he founded it "with three partners" but does not name them ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). Wikipedia names Murdock MacGregor as co-founder and says CBS Laboratories alumni Ron Davis, John Wistrand and Jim Catrambone joined early. That passage carries no inline citation, so treat the names as unconfirmed ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)). The same uncited passage says the company was first formed "to manufacture precast concrete housing," and tried an air conditioner "with no moving parts" before turning to cycling. No other source mentions this, and the NYT, road.cc and Altoona Mirror all start the story with the Bugger ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)).

**The name.** This is the company's favorite story. An employee, Peter Meyers, needed a phone line for the new business, so he "walked outside to a pay phone and placed an order. When asked what the company was called, Meyers is said to have peered at the name of a nearby neighborhood train station and said, 'Cannondale'" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). The station is Cannondale on the Metro-North Danbury branch in Wilton. Wikipedia also records a rival version from the company's own 1983 catalog: Meyers coined the name from a rusty cannon inscribed "dale" and the Cannon railroad crossing sign. Wikipedia calls this version "apocryphal" and "an inside joke among the company owners" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)).

![Cannondale station, Wilton, Connecticut](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Cannondale_station.jpg/1280px-Cannondale_station.jpg)

*Cannondale station in the Cannondale Historic District of Wilton, Connecticut, which gave the company its name. Photo: AirportExpert, [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cannondale_station.jpg), CC0.*

**The first office is disputed.** The NYT says "a loft above a pickle factory in Wilton" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). The Altoona Mirror, writing from Bedford, Pennsylvania where Cannondale later built its factory, says "the loft of the Cannondale train station" ([source](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2026/01/montgomery-cannondale-founder-cycling-pioneer)).

**The Bugger.** Cannondale's first cycling product was "a bicycle-towed trailer called the Bugger." road.cc notes dryly that it was a name that "didn't travel well" in Britain ([source](https://road.cc/content/feature/check-out-cannondales-first-ever-bike-1983-st-500-317553)). The origin story varies too:
- **The NYT version:** Montgomery saw "a cyclist struggling up a hill in Wilton, Conn., under the weight of a heavy backpack" and decided to build a trailer to carry the load ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).
- **The road.cc version:** the idea came "after a bike trip with his son" ([source](https://road.cc/content/feature/check-out-cannondales-first-ever-bike-1983-st-500-317553)).
- **Staying power:** by 1987 the Bugger was in its fourth generation ([source](https://road.cc/content/feature/check-out-cannondales-first-ever-bike-1983-st-500-317553)).

**The soft-goods decade.** Through the 1970s Cannondale made "cycling apparel, bike bags, tents, sleeping bags and even contracted and manufactured dog beds for the famous L.L. Bean brand." All of it came from "a repurposed shoe factory located in Bedford," Pennsylvania ([source](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2026/01/montgomery-cannondale-founder-cycling-pioneer)). The company did not build a bicycle until 1983.

### Part 2: The fat-tube revolution (1983 to 1993)

The turn to bicycles started with a letter. David Graham, an engineer at a Connecticut company that built submarines for the Navy, wrote to Montgomery suggesting bicycle frames be built from oversized aluminum. Cannondale "promptly hired him" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). His widow, Celia Montgomery, recalled: "Joe had experience from early years of his life where he crewed sailboats with masts made of oversized aluminum, so he understood the benefits of oversized aluminum structures" ([source](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2026/01/montgomery-cannondale-founder-cycling-pioneer)).

**1983: the ST-500.** Cannondale's first bicycle was the ST-500 touring bike, with an "oversized, TIG-welded aluminium frame" ([source](https://road.cc/content/feature/check-out-cannondales-first-ever-bike-1983-st-500-317553)). It sold for $495, more than $1,600 in today's money ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). The tubing was thin enough that, according to Cannondale's 50th anniversary book, if you pressed both sides of the largest tube with your thumbs, "you almost felt it give a little," the way a soda can would. It was "notoriously difficult to weld without burning holes in the material. It had to be done by hand" ([source](https://road.cc/content/feature/check-out-cannondales-first-ever-bike-1983-st-500-317553)).

Cannondale was not the first to use fat aluminum tubes: Escape Collective credits Gary Klein with pioneering the technology in 1974. Cannondale made "baseball-bat down tubes and chunky TIG welds into a conscious design statement all its own" ([source](https://escapecollective.com/cannondale-will-close-its-us-headquarters/)).

**1984: the first mountain bike.** The SM-500 cost $595 ([source](https://road.cc/content/feature/check-out-cannondales-first-ever-bike-1983-st-500-317553)). It had a 26-inch front wheel and a 24-inch rear wheel to help it roll over rocks and roots, and some critics called it "cycling's version of a mullet haircut" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

**The Montgomery family business.** Joe's son Scott joined in 1984. He was "co-president and vice president from 1984 to 2003 and was instrumental in starting Cannondale Japan and Cannondale Europe" ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2026/01/05/cannondale-founder-joe-montgomery-dies)).
- **Europe:** Cannondale Europe B.V., based in Oldenzaal, the Netherlands, was formed in 1989 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)).
- **Japan:** Cannondale Japan, based in Osaka, was formed in fiscal 1992 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)).
- **Conflicting dates:** the Altoona Mirror gives 1987 and 1991 ([source](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2026/01/montgomery-cannondale-founder-cycling-pioneer)). The SEC dates are for the legal entities.
- **Why Europe mattered:** it accounted for 35 to 41 percent of net sales in the mid-1990s ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)).

**Bedford, Pennsylvania.** Montgomery kept manufacturing in Bedford and flew there twice a week "aboard a corporate jet that he piloted." He often brought bike shop owners with him to make dealers feel like partners. A former designer said it was "like taking batting practice at Fenway Park" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). The bikes carried a "Handmade in USA" logo ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)).

**1990: across the Great Wall.** Cannondale built a bike that adventurer Kevin Foster rode more than 1,500 miles along the Great Wall of China. The prototype "had a seat that doubled as a tire pump and featured an automatic transmission that changed gears according to pedal pressure" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

**1992: Headshok.** Cannondale introduced the Headshok, a suspension fork built into an oversized head tube that rides on needle bearings. The early version was called "Delta V" ([source](https://vintagecannondale.com/info/headshok/), [source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)). Wikipedia also dates the "2.8" series frame to 1992, a design developed with computer-aided design and finite element analysis ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)).

**1993: "the Lamborghini of mountain bikes."** By 1993 the company had $100 million a year in revenue and 800 employees worldwide. The NYT called it "the Lamborghini of mountain bikes," with shock systems "more sophisticated than some people's first cars" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). That year also brought the Super V, a full-suspension mountain bike with no conventional main triangle, built around an oversized down tube and a high-pivot swingarm. Missy Giove won the 1994 downhill world championship on one ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)).

### Part 3: Wall Street and the golden age (1994 to 1999)

**The IPO, November 1994.** Cannondale's stock began trading on the Nasdaq National Market under the ticker **BIKE**. A second offering followed in September 1995 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)). The two 10-Ks disagree on the first trading day: the FY1996 10-K/A says 15 November 1994 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012396005293/0000950123-96-005293.txt)) and the FY1997 10-K says 16 November 1994 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)). Wikipedia's "1995 IPO" appears to confuse the IPO with the 1995 follow-on offering.

**Revenue climbed fast.** Net sales grew from $54.5 million in fiscal 1991 to $162.5 million in fiscal 1997 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)). By mid-1997 it employed 762 people full time in the US, 128 in Europe, 20 in Japan and 6 in Australia ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)).

**A new headquarters.** The company moved from Georgetown, Connecticut to a new $3.9 million headquarters and R&D building in Bethel, expected by the end of 1997 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000930795/000095012397008149/0000950123-97-008149.txt)).

**CAAD.** The "Cannondale Advanced Aluminum Design" label first appeared in 1996 and ran through generations, CAAD3 (1997) to CAAD9 (2007) ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)). By 2002 the company described "new generations of CAAD (Cannondale Advanced Aluminum Design) road and mountain bicycle frames" in its filings ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000093244002000407/cannondale10k_6-2002.txt)).

![Cannondale CAAD5](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bd/Cannondale_Caad_5.jpg/1280px-Cannondale_Caad_5.jpg)

*A Cannondale CAAD 5 road frame, the generation the Saeco team raced. Photo: PETE Alin., [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cannondale_Caad_5.jpg), CC BY-SA 3.0.*

**The Lefty.** The single-sided Lefty fork joined the Headshok as one of the brand's signature ideas ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2026/01/05/cannondale-founder-joe-montgomery-dies)). The NYT obituary describes it as "a single-legged fork, which provided a smooth ride" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

![Cannondale Lefty racing at Fort William, 2004](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1d/CannondaleLefty.JPG)

*A Siemens Mobile Cannondale Scalpel XC bike with a Lefty fork, racing at Fort William in 2004. Photo: Matt.S., [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CannondaleLefty.JPG), public domain.*

**A kooky streak.** In the 1990s Cannondale also built one-off concepts, among them a bike that "replaced a traditional front wheel with Rollerblade wheels for maximal aerodynamics" and the "Pong bike," made of computer-cut aluminum and resembling "a futuristic can opener." The cycling journalist Zapata Espinoza summed it up: "Cannondale started out with a kooky streak and they never lost it" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). Montgomery's own motto, as a former product marketing director remembered it, came with coins jingled in his pocket: "That's change. Change is good. We like change" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

### Part 4: The motorcycle years (1998 to 2003)

This is the chapter that cost the Montgomerys their company.

**The announcement, February 1998.** The FY1998 10-K said: "In February 1998, the Company announced plans to introduce a line of motocross motorcycles, currently in the prototype phase of development, for shipment in the summer of 1999" ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000095012398008534/0000950123-98-008534.txt)). The stated logic was that a motocross bike "enables the Company to capitalize on numerous core competencies including patented aluminum frame fabrication, patented Headshok needle bearing suspension and short-development cycles." The same filing describes a $7.5 million, 100,000 sq ft motorcycle plant in Bedford to be built by "an entity controlled by a director of the Company" ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000095012398008534/0000950123-98-008534.txt)).

Cycle World broke the news to riders in June 1998: "News flash! Bicycle manufacturer Cannondale is producing a motorcycle." It said the bike was positioned as a cross between Yamaha's YZ400F and a Husaberg FC 501 ([source](https://issues.cycleworld.com/article/1998/6/1/cannondale-to-make-motocrosser)). A month later a company spokesman spelled out the target: "We are addressing (what we see as) the weak points of Yamaha's YZ400F: difficulty starting, tall seat height and excessive weight" ([source](https://issues.cycleworld.com/article/1998/7/1/cannondale-revealed)).

**Three years of delays.** Cannondale showed red-and-black prototypes at the Cincinnati dealer show in 1998 and took orders, but "it took almost three years before those orders could be filled" ([source](https://roadbikeaction.com/the-bike-that-almost-killed-cannondale)).
- **The engine:** the original prototype used a Swedish Folan engine. Cannondale "threw away" that engine and had a purpose-built four-stroke designed in North Carolina ([source](https://roadbikeaction.com/the-bike-that-almost-killed-cannondale)).
- **The design:** dual overhead cams, four valves, and a reversed cylinder head with the intake in front and the exhaust at the rear ([source](https://dirtwheelsmag.com/history-cannondale-quads)).

**Launch, May 2000.** Per the company's 10-K, "In May 2000, we entered the motorsports industry with the commercial introduction of the Cannondale MX400... and followed in February 2001 with shipments of our FX400 ATV." The engine was a "proprietary liquid-cooled, electric-start, 432 cc four-stroke engine with a unique reversed cylinder head," and it was fuel injected ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000093244002000407/cannondale10k_6-2002.txt)).
- **2003 motorcycles:** X440, E440, E440R, E440A, C440R and S440.
- **2003 ATVs:** Moto 440, Blaze 440, Speed 440, Glamis 440 and the Cannibal 440, which ATV Magazine named "Best Sport ATV" in March 2002 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000093244002000407/cannondale10k_6-2002.txt)).

**Reliability problems.** "The first arrival of the dirt bikes didn't get glowing reviews because of handling and suspension issues," and the quads had "some of the same durability problems." Cannondale responded with "recalls and free updates" ([source](https://dirtwheelsmag.com/history-cannondale-quads)). The MX400 frame "had to bulge in the front to make room for the electric starter motor, which wouldn't work when the engine got hot" ([source](https://roadbikeaction.com/the-bike-that-almost-killed-cannondale)). Motocross Action's own retelling adds that after the bankruptcy, Joe Montgomery "pointed the finger at MXA's bike test as the cause of the MX400's failure" ([source](https://roadbikeaction.com/the-bike-that-almost-killed-cannondale)).

**The money.** The FY2002 10-K segment table is blunt ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000093244002000407/cannondale10k_6-2002.txt)):

| Fiscal year | Motorsports sales | Motorsports operating loss | Bicycle operating income |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | $0.06M | ($8.5M) | $7.6M |
| 2001 | $5.3M | ($16.8M) | $9.3M |
| 2002 | $22.0M | ($21.4M) | $9.2M |

Over those three years the motorsports division lost about $46.7 million at the operating line (our sum of the filing's figures), against bicycle profits of about $26 million. The company admitted it was realizing "negative margins on the sale of most of our motorsports products," and it posted net losses of $20.3 million in fiscal 2001 and $15.4 million in fiscal 2002 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000093244002000407/cannondale10k_6-2002.txt)). In July 2002 it sold $25 million of senior notes and warrants to Pegasus Partners II. That was the first appearance of the firm that would end up owning the company ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/930795/000093244002000407/cannondale10k_6-2002.txt)).

**The brink, December 2002.** The Associated Press reported 600 manufacturing workers furloughed. It said the motorsports move "has caused resentment among some long-time employees as the division has drained millions from the company's coffers and sucked resources from its bicycle division" ([source](https://bikebiz.com/cannondale-is-on-the-brink-claim-industry-analysts/)).

**Chapter 11, 29 January 2003.** Cannondale filed for Chapter 11 on 29 January 2003, and Pegasus agreed to act as "stalking horse" bidder ([source](https://sgbonline.com/?p=4306)). The filing listed $114 million in assets and $105 million in debts, in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Connecticut ([source](https://powersportsbusiness.com/features/2003/03/31/cannondale-goes-to-a-creditor/)). By May 2003 all activity at the Bedford motorcycle factory had been suspended, after "approximately 5000 machines" were sold ([source](https://issues.cycleworld.com/article/2003/5/1/death-of-a-dirtbike)).

**The post-mortem, in the Montgomerys' words.** Scott Montgomery told Cycle World in 2003: "Hindsight is always 20/20... We recognized that as a public company we needed to increase sales and keep the company growing. We felt the off-road motorcycle and ATV markets were poised for a lot of growth. We knew it was a difficult task, but we thought we were going to be able to do it." An unnamed industry insider put it more simply: "They just bit off more than they could chew" ([source](https://issues.cycleworld.com/article/2003/5/1/death-of-a-dirtbike)). On the outcome for the family, Scott said: "The shareholders are wiped out, myself and my father included" ([source](https://powersportsbusiness.com/features/2003/03/31/cannondale-goes-to-a-creditor/)). Twenty-three years later the NYT obituary quoted Espinoza calling it "the motorcycle debacle that lost him his company," and Scott's verdict on his father: he "would have rather pushed the envelope on technology, and lead and crash and burn, than to be timid and afraid" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

**What happened to the motorcycles.** Pegasus did "not intend to operate the motorsports division" ([source](https://sgbonline.com/?p=4306)). The assets split three ways:
- **Holding company:** Pegasus put the motorsports assets into Moto Assets Acquisition Corp. ([source](https://powersportsbusiness.com/features/2003/10/20/dinli-buys-cannondale-secrets/)).
- **Parts:** ATK Motorcycles took "all remaining Cannondale Motorsports inventory," valued "at over $10MM," and shipped parts through the old dealer network ([source](https://motocrossactionmag.com/atk-buys-out-cannondale-motorsports-inventory/)). ATK also sold fixes, including a piston that "lasted three times longer" ([source](https://dirtwheelsmag.com/history-cannondale-quads)).
- **Designs:** Taiwan's Dinli Metal Industrial Co. bought the motorsports intellectual property in October 2003, excluding the Cannondale name ([source](https://powersportsbusiness.com/features/2003/10/20/dinli-buys-cannondale-secrets/)).

No freely licensed photo of an MX400 or a Cannondale ATV exists on Wikimedia Commons, so none is shown here.

### Part 5: Bankruptcy and the Pegasus years (2003 to 2008)

On 28 March 2003 the bankruptcy court approved the sale of substantially all of Cannondale's assets to affiliates of Pegasus Capital. The company warned there would be "insufficient funds" for shareholders and that "equity has no value" ([source](https://sgbonline.com/?p=4306)). Sources disagree on the price:
- **Powersports Business (2003):** Pegasus agreed to pay $55 million for the bicycle assets, against an asking price of $57.2 million ([source](https://powersportsbusiness.com/features/2003/03/31/cannondale-goes-to-a-creditor/)).
- **Bicycle Retailer (later):** "about $58 million" ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2026/01/05/cannondale-founder-joe-montgomery-dies)).
- **Official terms:** never disclosed.

The bicycle business carried on as Cannondale Bicycle Corporation ([source](https://powersportsbusiness.com/features/2003/10/20/dinli-buys-cannondale-secrets/)). Matt Mannelly became president in 2003 and CEO in 2005 ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2008/07/29/mannelly-leaving-cannondale)). After the bankruptcy, Joe Montgomery went on to found a software company that handled billing and medical records ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)).

### Part 6: Dorel, and the end of Made in USA (2008 to 2021)

**Dorel buys Cannondale, February 2008.** Dorel Industries, the Canadian juvenile-products and bicycle group that already owned Schwinn and Mongoose through Pacific Cycle, agreed to buy Cannondale and the Sugoi apparel brand from a Pegasus affiliate for "US$190 to US$200 million," depending on Cannondale's earnings for the year ending June 2008 ([source](https://pedalmag.com/dorel-acquires-cannondale-and-creates-ibd-division/), [source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2008/02/04/dorel-buys-cannondale)). Dorel created the "Cannondale Sports Group" to sell Cannondale, Sugoi and GT through independent dealers ([source](https://pedalmag.com/dorel-acquires-cannondale-and-creates-ibd-division/)). By April 2009 it had been renamed Cycling Sports Group ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2009/04/02/cannondales-us-manufacturing-ending)). Mannelly's departure was announced in July 2008, with a transition period until September ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2008/07/29/mannelly-leaving-cannondale)).

**The end of US frame-building, 2009 to 2010.** On 1 April 2009 Dorel announced it was "shifting Bedford's operations away from bicycle frame manufacturing by 2010, and to Taichung, Taiwan. All Cannondale 2011 model year bikes will be sourced from Asia" ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2009/04/02/cannondales-us-manufacturing-ending)).
- **Jobs:** Bedford's 300 jobs would fall to 100 ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2009/04/02/cannondales-us-manufacturing-ending)). The final round of 92 layoffs was set for July 2010 ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2010/05/03/cannondale-completes-bedford-plant-layoffs)).
- **First steps abroad:** two years earlier Cannondale had already "turned to Asia for the first time to source entry-level aluminum bikes" ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/north-america/2009/04/02/cannondales-us-manufacturing-ending)).

**Back to Wilton, 2013.** Connecticut gave a $3 million loan to help the company set up a new headquarters of about 50,000 sq ft in Wilton's I-Park, with 75 new jobs, because it had "outgrown its current location in Bethel" ([source](https://portal.ct.gov/DECD/Press-Room/Press-Releases/2013/2013-05-10-Gov-Malloy-Cannondale-Expands-Operations-Establishes-Wilton-Headquarters)). Bicycle Retailer confirms the move back to Wilton came in 2013 ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2026/10/21/cannondale-closing-connecticut-headquarters-consolidating-other-pon-brands)).

**Bedford closes, 2014.** In January 2014 Dorel said it would close the Bedford assembly and testing facility and move R&D from Bethel to Wilton, with about 100 layoffs ([source](https://bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2014/01/23/dorel-restructures-bike-business)). Wikipedia says the facility shut in 2015 ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)).

**Pon agrees to buy, October 2021.** Dorel agreed to sell its whole Dorel Sports division to Pon Holdings for $810 million in cash. The division held Cannondale, Schwinn, Mongoose, GT, Charge, Caloi and KidTrax ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2021/10/11/pon-agrees-buy-dorel-sports-810-million)). The deal closed on 4 January 2022, valued at about C$1.03 billion, with roughly $735 million in net proceeds to Dorel ([source](https://bikebiz.com/dorel-completes-sale-of-sports-segment-to-pon-holdings), [source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2022/01/04/dorel-completes-sale-dorel-sports-pon-holdings)). Pon's bike group already owned Gazelle, Kalkhoff, Focus, Santa Cruz, Cervélo, Urban Arrow and Juliana, among others ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2021/10/11/pon-agrees-buy-dorel-sports-810-million)).

### Part 7: Pon and the long consolidation (2022 to 2026)

- **Nov 2022: one global structure.** Managing director Marco Kind, formerly of Pon's Focus brand, announced a "global unified organizational structure that will eliminate regional general managers" ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2022/11/11/cannondale-announces-new-organizational-structure)).
- **Apr 2024: small cuts.** Cannondale cut its workforce by "less than 1%" while moving from a multi-regional to a unified global structure ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2024/04/30/cannondale-reduces-workforce-part-reorganization)).
- **May 2025: leadership of record.** Kind was still Cannondale's managing director, and Nick Hage was "SVP General Manager, Cycling Sports Group" ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/announcements/2025/05/14/peopleforbikes-coalition-board-directors-meets)).
- **2025: a multi-brand strategy.** Pon.Bike introduced an "integrated multi-brand approach" ([source](https://road.cc/news/120-employees-laid-off-as-cannondale-production-facility-to-close)). Pon reported bicycle revenue of "around 2 billion euros" for 2025, and Christian Dahlheim replaced Janus Smalbraak as Pon Holdings CEO on 1 April 2026 ([source](https://www.bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2026/04/02/cannondale-owner-pon-holdings-reports-sales-2b-euros-bikes-last-year)).
- **2026: the unwinding.** Pon announced the phased closure of the Almelo factory, ended the factory MTB team after 2026 and, today, announced the closure of Wilton. Escape Collective calls it the latest "sharp turn in Cannondale's up-and-down 55-year history" ([source](https://escapecollective.com/cannondale-will-close-its-us-headquarters/)). Details are in [Latest news](#latest-news-2024-to-october-2026).

### Racing: the bikes that won

**Volvo-Cannondale (1994 to 2002): mountain biking's first big mainstream sponsor.** Pedal Magazine called Volvo "mountain bike racing's first major mainstream sponsor." At the 1994 Worlds in Vail the team won four golds, two silvers and a bronze ([source](https://pedalmag.com/volvocannondale-era-comes-to-an-end/)). Volvo ended the deal in 2002 after "a global review of Volvo's overall sponsorship strategy" ([source](https://canadiancyclist.com/news/5695/volvo-to-end-cycling-sponsorship)).
- **Roster:** Missy Giove, Alison Sydor, Myles Rockwell, Brian Lopes, Tinker Juarez, Cadel Evans (later a Tour de France winner), Christoph Sauser, Anne-Caroline Chausson and Cédric Gracia ([source](https://canadiancyclist.com/news/5695/volvo-to-end-cycling-sponsorship), [source](https://road.cc/offroad/feature/cannondale-factory-racing-pulls-the-plug-marking-the-end-of-a-glorious-era)).
- **Tally in 2002:** "two Olympic Medals, nine World Championships, fourteen World Cup overall titles, and eighty-five World Cup victories" ([source](https://canadiancyclist.com/news/5695/volvo-to-end-cycling-sponsorship)).
- **Tally in 2026:** road.cc gives the same Volvo-Cannondale team a bigger haul, "11 World Championships, 89 World Cup wins, 17 overall titles." The two counts conflict and neither explains its method ([source](https://road.cc/offroad/feature/cannondale-factory-racing-pulls-the-plug-marking-the-end-of-a-glorious-era)).

**Cannondale Factory Racing.** The factory team carried on after Volvo:
- **Marco Aurelio Fontana:** his Olympedia record lists a Cannondale affiliation and a cross-country bronze at London 2012 ([source](https://www.olympedia.org/athletes/113246)).
- **Henrique Avancini:** spent eight years on the team. He was 2018 marathon world champion and won the Nové Město World Cup in 2020 ([source](https://www.cannondale.com/en-ca/blog/looking-back-a-farewell-to-ave)).
- **The end:** in June 2026 Cannondale said the 2026 World Cup season would be CFR's last "after three decades of competition," replacing it with "Cannondale Rogues," a community and influencer program ([source](https://www.endurancesportswire.com/cannondale-to-shut-down-factory-race-program/)).

**Saeco and Mario Cipollini.** Mario Cipollini was "the first rider to win a stage of the Tour de France while riding a Cannondale bike," in stage one of the 1997 Tour ([source](https://www.ridemedia.com.au/articles/cannondale-bikes-in-the-pro-peloton-2004-2026/)). At the 1999 Tour he "won four consecutive stages aboard a Cannondale racing bike" ([source](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/02/01/metro/joe-montgomery-who-made-bicycles-lighter-dies-86)). A common error is worth correcting: his 2002 world championship came on a Specialized E5, not a Cannondale ([source](https://velo.outsideonline.com/road/road-training/retro-tech-mario-cipollinis-world-champion-specialized-e5-bikes/)).

**Simoni and Cunego.** Gilberto Simoni's 2003 Giro d'Italia win for Saeco appeared in Cannondale TV spots that aired during OLN's Tour coverage that July ([source](https://bikebiz.com/cannondale-airs-tv-ad-on-olns-tour-de-france-coverage/)). Wikipedia says Simoni won the 2003 Giro on a CAAD7 and Damiano Cunego the 2004 Giro on a CAAD8 ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannondale)).

**Liquigas: Basso, Nibali and Sagan.** In 2010, Ivan Basso won the Giro d'Italia and Vincenzo Nibali the Vuelta a España, both on the SuperSix. In 2012 its successor, the SuperSix EVO, carried Peter Sagan to the Tour de France points jersey "in its first outing" ([source](https://bikeradar.com/features/tech/cannondale-supersix-evolution)). The first SuperSix, in 2007, was "Cannondale's first full carbon fibre bike" ([source](https://bikeradar.com/features/tech/cannondale-supersix-evolution)). Sagan won the green jersey three years running. In 2014, on the Cannondale team, he scored 431 points, 149 ahead of Alexander Kristoff ([source](https://enrsi.stvr.sk/articles/news/32543/peter-sagan-wins-green-jersey-at-tour-de-france-third-time-in-a-row)).

**The American era: Garmin, Drapac, EF.** In 2015 the Cannondale team merged with Jonathan Vaughters' US-registered squad as Cannondale-Garmin, ending the run of Italian-registered teams ([source](https://www.ridemedia.com.au/articles/cannondale-bikes-in-the-pro-peloton-2004-2026/)). Three results stand out:
- **Rigoberto Urán, 2017 Tour:** he won stage 9 with a broken rear derailleur ([source](https://www.espn.com/endurance/story/_/id/20088559/2017-tour-de-france-unexpected-rise-colombia-rigoberto-uran)) and finished second overall for Cannondale-Drapac, 54 seconds behind Chris Froome. Vaughters afterwards: "Nobody else knew it, but we came to the race to win the Tour de France" ([source](https://cyclingweekly.com/news/racing/nobody-else-knew-uran-came-win-tour-de-france-343436)).
- **Alberto Bettiol, 2019 Tour of Flanders:** he had begun his career with Cannondale Pro Cycling in 2014, and won the race for EF as his first professional victory ([source](https://www.cannondale.com/fr-FR/blog/the-first-win-a-flanders-retrospective)).
- **The EF deal:** in 2023 EF and Cannondale announced "an even longer term partnership," one "without an end date, in fact," and planned an EF women's team with Cannondale as title partner ([source](https://www.efprocycling.com/culture/cannondale-and-ef-pro-cycling-extend-partnership/)).

## Open Questions

- **The 2003 sale price.** No primary source (an 8-K or the bankruptcy docket) was opened. $55M and $58M both circulate.
- **Lefty introduction year.** A FY2000 company filing reportedly refers to a "2000 model year Lefty," but that filing was not re-checked for this article, so the year is left out of the text.
- **The other three founders, and the pre-cycling business.** The NYT says "three partners" without names. Wikipedia's names, and its precast-concrete origin story, are uncited.
- **The new North American office.** It is undecided per Cannondale, and Santa Cruz per Bicycle Retailer. Watch for a follow-up announcement.
- **Leadership now.** No named Cannondale president or CEO for 2026 was found beyond managing director Marco Kind (last confirmed May 2025). The CSG North America SVP/GM role is on the WARN list, but no article names who leaves.
- **EF women's team bike sponsor for 2026.** Not separately confirmed.
- **Recalls since 2024.** None found, but the CPSC manufacturer listing blocked automated access, so absence is not proven.
- **Fontana's 2012 bronze.** Widely reported to have come after his seatpost broke on the last lap, but no opened source confirmed the detail, so it is not claimed.
- **A sale of Cannondale by Pon.** No rumor was found; coverage frames 2026 as consolidation inside Pon, not divestment.
